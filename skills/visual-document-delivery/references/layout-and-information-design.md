# Layout and Information Design

## Reading order

Lead with the answer and tell the reader how to use the document. In the first 20% of a substantial document, include a compact contents block, overview, route map, or other orientation device.

Use this rhythm for a complex chapter:

1. conclusion in a short paragraph;
2. evidence in a compact table or card;
3. the visual next to the relationship it explains;
4. interpretation, fact boundary, and limitations;
5. action, decision, or `Open` item.

## Hierarchy

- One title names the deliverable and reader.
- Major headings divide decisions or stages, not arbitrary page length.
- Subheadings answer a reader question.
- Captions explain what to notice, evidence status, source, and limitations.
- Reuse one direction, color meaning, line style, heading system, and source format throughout the document.

## Tables

Use tables for short fields, exact mapping, and comparison. Keep prose outside the table when a cell needs more than a few short sentences. On narrow surfaces, split a wide table into cards or two smaller tables instead of shrinking type.

## Diagrams

- Give every node a concrete role in the relationship.
- Use verbs on arrows when the transition is not obvious.
- Show branches, failure paths, and terminal states that matter to the source logic.
- Keep labels short enough to read at normal desktop zoom.
- Add a nearby paragraph for nuance rather than filling the diagram with prose.
- Preserve the source structure for any requested 1:1 workflow reproduction.

## Professional visual rhythm

Avoid uninterrupted walls of text and image walls. Alternate concise conclusions, evidence, visuals, and action blocks according to the content—not a decorative pattern. Use whitespace to separate reasoning units, not to inflate length.

## Accessibility and portability

- Do not rely on color alone to communicate evidence status or route priority.
- Use sufficient contrast and readable labels.
- Supply meaningful captions or alt text where the format supports them.
- Ensure the document still makes sense when printed or exported to PDF.
